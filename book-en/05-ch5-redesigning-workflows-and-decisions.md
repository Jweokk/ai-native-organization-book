# Chapter 5: Redesigning Workflows and Decisions

## 5.1 Redesign First, Automate Second

In 1990, Michael Hammer published a famous article in the *Harvard Business Review* titled "Don't Automate, Obliterate." His core point is even more applicable in the AI era: **before automating a bad process, ask why that process exists in the first place.**

The first principle of AI-native workflow redesign is: **not "give everyone a chatbot," but redesign how work flows, how decisions get made, how data is captured, how humans review risk, and what AI is allowed to assist with or act on.**

The fundamental difference between an automation-first organization and an AI-native organization:

| Question | Automation-first organization | AI-native organization |
|------|--------------|--------------|
| Starting point | Where can we add AI? | **What work should change?** |
| Success metric | How many tasks were automated / how many prompts were used | Whether the outcomes of the whole workflow improved |
| Main risk | Faster rework | Discovering redesign gaps before scaling |
| Data approach | Use existing data | Redesign data capture around decisions |
| Human role | Post-hoc reviewer | Responsible designer, supervisor, and owner of exceptions |
| Scaling pattern | More tools and pilots | A repeatable workflow-redesign loop |

## 5.2 Nine Key Workflow Redesigns

**1. Start from business outcomes, not AI tools.** Outcomes must be specific enough to guide trade-offs: "30% fewer supplier-invoice exceptions without adding approval risk" beats "use AI in finance." Technology choices follow workflow intent.

**2. Map the real workflow before automating the official one.** The official process diagram and how employees actually work (inbox shortcuts / private spreadsheets / informal escalation paths / exception handling) exist side by side as two versions. Delete steps before automating: merge duplicate approvals, turn low-value checks into rules, give exceptions an owner. **Don't teach AI to preserve unnecessary work.**

**3. Redesign intake so AI gets better input.** Many AI projects die at the door: expecting AI to classify, route, or decide on messy input that was never designed for it. Required fields, structured options, validation at capture points, automatic enrichment from trusted systems, and flagging low-confidence cases. **Don't blame the model for weak input before redesigning the front door.**

**4. Separate rules / automation / AI assistance / agents / humans.** Not every step needs AI: stable rules → rule automation; system handoffs → workflow automation; language-intensive review → AI assistance; pattern recognition → AI models; low-risk multi-step tasks → constrained agents; high-accountability decisions → human plus AI support. Prevent two errors: using AI where rules suffice, and delegating high-risk decisions just because AI can generate confident-sounding suggestions.

**5. Build a workflow data foundation, not an abstract data lake.** Ask "have we captured the data needed to make the workflow better?" rather than "do we have data?" Record when humans override AI suggestions and why — the best learning comes from the redesigned workflow itself.

**6. Define decision rights before AI systems act.** If humans aren't clear on decision rights, AI is even less safe. The decision rights table is the tool that operationalizes this:

| Scenario | What AI may do | What humans retain |
|------|------------|------------|
| Classification / routing | AI acts on high confidence | Humans review low-confidence cases |
| Drafting | AI recommends drafts | Humans approve sensitive content; template and tone control |
| Low-value refunds | AI acts within limits | Humans review over-limit cases; thresholds + logs + fraud checks |
| Contract changes | AI assists only | Humans decide |
| Risk escalation | AI acts immediately | Humans investigate and close |

**7. Replace manual handoffs with event-driven work and review queues.** **Stop treating people as glue between systems.** Trigger → create record → enrich → apply rules → AI classify/summarize → route → exceptions enter a visible review queue (with context/priority/owner/deadline/escalation path). Don't make handoffs invisible; make important handoffs observable.

**8. Measure the whole workflow, not AI tasks.** Task-level time savings mislead (drafting got faster but review got slower; classification got faster but the queue got wrong). Metrics: cycle time, first-time-right rate, exception rate and exception age, review time, satisfaction, cost per completed case, rework rate, AI confidence and coverage, escalation quality, incidents/control violations. **After the pilot, ask: did the work improve, or did AI just make one step look faster?**

**9. Create a repeatable 90-day workflow-redesign cadence.**

**Processes themselves can be redesigned: a review system is not the same thing as a PR.** Amp, a 20-person team, pushes straight to main without pull requests and still passes SOC 2<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#8-64">[8-64]</a></sup> — auditors never cared about the git workflow; they care that changes are authorized, tested, approved, and recorded. Their control stack: push permissions restricted by business function, signed commits (GitHub verified), fully automated CI validation, and an audit trail linking commits to business context. The scaling insight: risk is not uniform inside an enterprise, and calibrating every system to "the scariest one" is hidden waste — first ask "what risk is the PR actually managing," then decide where it is needed.

**Break the process into three buckets instead of adding AI to every step.** Varick (which deploys agents for enterprises with more than $500 million in revenue) sorts every redesigned step into one of three categories<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#8-89">[8-89]</a></sup>: **deterministic** (if X then Y, no judgment — plain code: cheap, auditable, immune to hallucination, and fast); **agentic** (thousands of past examples of human judgment with recorded outcomes, at low enough risk, such as deal-desk approvals or GL coding — use the model for judgment, especially when the output is yes/no, route-to-this-team, or flag/don't-flag); and **human-in-the-loop** (too risky or too little history — assemble the evidence for a person so they can decide in 30 seconds instead of spending 40 minutes digging through email). On that basis they compressed a global company's monthly reconciliation of more than 300 bank accounts from 25 steps to 3 agents plus 2 human checkpoints. **The money is not in making each step faster; it is in deleting the queues and handoffs** — Hammer's 1990 example remains the best footnote: an insurance application sat in process for 22 days while actual working time was 17 minutes, so doubling the speed of those 17 minutes saves eight and a half<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#8-89">[8-89]</a></sup>. The results across their client deployments: month-end close cut from 18–22 days to 7–9, AP exception handling down from 600–800 invoices a month to under 50, and payroll corrections down 90%<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#8-89">[8-89]</a></sup>.

**Four engineering disciplines that cannot be reordered.** AI-native engineering practice compresses into one sequence<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#8-90">[8-90]</a></sup>: make **specifications executable** (tickets spell out acceptance criteria, constraints, dependencies, and failure cases — the bar being that hand it to a new engineer or an agent, and both produce a roughly similar first plan without a clarification call); treat **context as an asset** (architecture notes, coding standards, known edge cases, and acceptable patterns live where the work happens, not in a Slack thread); **build verification before expanding autonomy** (deterministic tests, static analysis, policy checks on sensitive changes, and bounded tool permissions with audit trails all come before broader automation — this is earning the right to accelerate); and make **review the center of leverage** (once generation is cheap, what is scarce is judging correctness, architectural fit, and long-term maintainability, so experienced engineers should concentrate there rather than rewriting by hand). The rollout is staged: weeks 1–4 cover specifications, context, and verification; weeks 5–12 add review, continuous QA, and standard delivery paths; only from the second quarter onward do end-to-end ownership, system-quality metrics, feedback loops, and engineer capability building.

## 5.3 The 90-Day Redesign Cadence (The Actionable Core)

- **Days 1–15: Select** — a process with visible pain + measurable value + sufficient volume + controllable risk;
- **Days 16–30: Map** — the real workflow / systems / data / decisions / handoffs / approvals / exceptions / owners / current performance;
- **Days 31–45: Redesign** — delete steps, clarify ownership, define decision rights, standardize intake, improve data capture, assign people / rules / automation / AI / agents to each step;
- **Days 46–60: Build** — workflow / integrations / prompts / access control / review queues / dashboards / logs / training;
- **Days 61–75: Pilot** — real users run real cases inside safe boundaries, measuring the whole workflow;
- **Days 76–90: Govern and scale** — kill / fix / scale, record the lessons, pick the next process.

## 5.4 Eight Common Mistakes

① Treating AI access as transformation; ② automating before simplifying (AI accelerates work of the wrong shape); ③ using AI where rules suffice; ④ ignoring exception work (the real value lies in handling messy cases); ⑤ measuring prompts instead of outcomes; ⑥ excluding employees from the redesign; ⑦ giving agents power without control (requires thresholds / logs / permissions / review queues / rollback / named owners); ⑧ redesigning the whole company at once (start with one process).

## 5.5 Dividing Labor Between Humans and AI: From "Full Replacement" to "Three-Layer Routing"

Beyond workflow redesign, designing the boundary of human–AI division of labor is the core of decision redesign.

**Anthropic's internal evidence gives a key number: most employees believe that only 0–20% of work can be "fully delegated" to AI<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#8-40">[8-40]</a></sup>.** In other words, the human–AI division of labor in an AI-native organization is "supervised collaboration," not "hands-off automation." The number of autonomous actions Claude Code could take before human intervention was needed grew from about 10 to about 20 — this "intervention threshold" is dynamic and moves as model capability improves.

The hybrid-model **three-layer routing** that Klarna distilled from its customer-service reversal is currently the most mature template for dividing labor:

- **Layer 1: AI handles routine volume end to end** (targeting 60–70% of traffic) — order status, basic returns, FAQs;
- **Layer 2: AI-assisted human** (AI drafts, human reviews and sends, 20–25%);
- **Layer 3: Human-led high-value / escalation scenarios** (5–15% of traffic, but the greatest retention impact) — complex billing disputes, fraud, account cancellation.

**Lesson**: Klarna's first round of AI customer service only did Layer 1 (and did it aggressively); overall volume-based metrics (resolution rate, first-response time) looked good, but CSAT for complex interactions dropped significantly<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#7-14">[7-14]</a></sup> and the repeat-contact rate rose — the overall average masked a quality collapse in retention-critical scenarios. **You must track satisfaction for "complex/escalation interactions" separately.**

**Treating AI as a compiler leads to frustration; treating it as a collaborator works.** One engineer's observation is worth remembering: code gives certainty (same input, same output; a difference is a bug), humans give uncertainty (a colleague may understand intent and produce a better result), and AI sits between the two — rather than treating it as a compiler that precisely executes instructions, collaborate with it the way you would lead: share context, explain the desired outcome, set boundaries, respond to feedback. The core shift is investing in **expressing intent** — explaining why the work matters, what a good result looks like, and where judgment is needed. "The technology is new; leadership is not." This is also the underlying capability for the "human–AI division of labor design" in Chapter 8.

There is also an underappreciated ordering problem on the cost side: **choose the router first, then the model.** Most teams building agents pick the model first and the architecture second — backwards. Model choice should be the last decision, not the first. What actually determines cost and quality is the router: a small piece of code that decides which tier of model handles each request. Routing has three layers: a **skill classifier** turns the raw request into a concrete operation (intent recognition), the **router** decides which tier executes it (reading only the classification label plus a few features: complexity, context size, historical success rate), and a **model selector** picks the cheapest model within the tier that clears a confidence threshold. The classifier is a language problem; the router is a scheduling problem — conflating them buries model choice inside the prompt and kills the ability to A/B different models on the same operation. Get routing right and 70–80% of traffic can run on local models or async models; Coinbase used "better defaults, routing, and caching" to halve AI spend<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#8-80">[8-80]</a></sup> while token usage grew exponentially. Ask one question first: how much work actually needs a real-time answer? Once you can queue work, the answer is often: very little.

**Why the human–AI division of labor must be computed end to end.** ByteByteGo's interview with Meta's ads-systems architect supplies a denominator that is easy to get wrong: **only 20–30% of an engineer's time goes into writing code**, while review, testing, coordination, and governance consume the other 70–80% — and that is exactly where the bottlenecks form. After that team systematically removed the surrounding bottlenecks, it achieved a 1.8–2.4x delivery speedup over six months<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#7-75">[7-75]</a></sup>. Their division-of-labor pattern is "humans set direction, AI executes, humans review results": people set the plan and success criteria, AI implements and self-verifies, iterates on its own until the criteria are met, and humans approve the final output — yielding a 40–50% speedup over the previous development loop for one large team, while another team reached human-on-the-loop in under four months, against the 6–12 months such transitions usually take<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#7-75">[7-75]</a></sup>. This also corrects the figure at the start of 5.5: the share of work that can be fully delegated to AI remains low, but **what actually determines organizational returns is whether the other 70% beyond generation gets redesigned too.**

## 5.6 Corroboration with Chinese Practice

The three common features from Chapter 2 (AI-informed decisions replacing experience-based decisions, unifying business flow and workflow, making expertise reproducible) all have concrete implementation mechanisms in this chapter:

- **AI-informed decisions**: Huawei (Chinese: 华为) makes intelligent analytics a default capability of its data platform — "you don't go query it; the system gives it to you automatically";
- **Unifying business flow and workflow**: Feishu (Chinese: 飞书; Lark internationally) connects IM, documents, and business flows, with AI intervening in project nodes in real time;
- **Making expertise reproducible**: Transn's (Chinese: 传神) AI automatically records decision logic and updates business rules, turning personal experience into organizational assets — at the same time, Yang Renbin's "decision model index" from Chapter 4 (modeling the decision layer's judgment methods as a callable system) and the decision rights table are two sides of the same coin: one is the governance side, the other the cognition side.
- **Governing AI coding**: Meituan (Chinese: 美团) reapplied its "eval agent" methodology to managing AI coding — first "human–human alignment" (one dictator beats ten democrats), then "human–AI alignment" (constraints written as AI Rules, with a human–AI agreement rate held at 90% or above; reverse the order and the AI Rules become empty words). AI code review uses higher-tier models to review lower-tier output, cross-vendor mutual review, plus a Pre-PR mechanism; technical debt is dissolved as "ride-along work" attached to business requirements, with zero dedicated scheduling. This system keeps a codebase of 310,000 lines — about 90% AI-generated — maintainable<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#8-83">[8-83]</a></sup>. It also rewrites the definition of experience value: from "being able to see everything" to "knowing what matters."
**A code-generation rate above 30%, with essentially unchanged delivery efficiency — Kuaishou turned that gap into a three-year retrospective.** In 2026 Kuaishou's R&D efficiency committee published the AI research-and-development paradigm shift of its 10,000-person engineering organization: from 2023 to 2024 it first pushed platformization, digitization, and lean operations until tool penetration exceeded 95% and process automation 94%, lifting per-capita requirement throughput by 41.57%; during "Intelligence 1.0" (June 2024 to June 2025) it focused on individual developer productivity, reaching an AI code-generation rate above 30% — while organization-level delivery efficiency stayed "essentially flat"; since July 2025 it has moved to "Intelligence 2.0," retargeting the effort from individual speed to organization-wide throughput along an explicit ladder of L1 AI assistance (Copilot) → L2 AI collaboration (Agent) → L3 AI autonomy (Agentic)<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#7-77">[7-77]</a></sup>. The takeaway is a formula any organization can copy: **using AI coding tools ≠ individual productivity gains ≠ organizational productivity gains** — for individual gains to propagate, you first have to know which step is the bottleneck (Kuaishou built an efficiency model specifically to locate it), otherwise you just pile output in front of the slowest workstation.

**Faster coding is not faster delivery.** A widely cited engineering analysis puts it concretely: in the morning AI generates eight features at once; in the afternoon eight pull requests wait for review and the test environment queues up; three days later the business is still confirming requirements, security has just started checking permissions, and operations says this week's release window has closed. Management sees "a week of code generated in a day"; the business sees requirements that are no less late, with more rework and coordination. OpenAI's 2026 agent-first software engineering experiment — a team starting from an empty repository, using Codex to generate application logic, tests, CI, documentation and observability code, estimated at roughly one tenth of the time of handwritten code for one internal product — is often summarized as "shipping 10x faster," but the original stresses that engineers shift to designing environments, expressing intent, and building feedback loops<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#7-79">[7-79]</a></sup>. A second piece of evidence comes from benchmarking: change the harness for the same model — the software system that manages its tools, context and task execution — and task success rates move within ±2% on SWE-bench Lite and about ±5% on Terminal-Bench 2.0, while cost varies by 2-5x; Claude Code costs about 2.0x the minimal open-source harness Pi on SWE-bench Lite, with an initial context more than 10x larger. **Changing the harness before changing the model is often cheaper than upgrading the model; and the delivery bottleneck is always the slowest step, never coding.**<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#7-78">[7-78]</a></sup>



Half a year later, engineers at two more companies spelled out the same path in more detail. A roundtable between the R&D leads of Kuaishou (Chinese: 快手) and Xiaohongshu (Chinese: 小红书) quantified the contradiction in a single set of numbers: AI's code contribution had reached 60% and engineers felt roughly 40% faster, yet requirements-delivery efficiency and organizational R&D throughput did not rise in step<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#8-121">[8-121]</a></sup>. The attribution fell in three places — the organization still used the traditional R&D division of labor, information kept leaking as it passed among roles, and business/domain/engineering knowledge had not been consolidated and connected; in other words, the bottleneck was not the tool but collaboration and the knowledge system. The paths they describe match: Kuaishou's fix is to clarify tasks proactively and define an SDD plan up front, and downstream to visualize code changes and run cross-agent review, using SubAgents to relieve the context-window limit, moving gradually from Human in the Loop to Human on the Loop; Xiaohongshu built its own context framework and agentic system, folding expert experience into model control to balance generalization against output quality. A counterintuitive observation is that **small and mid-sized teams land it faster** — they can adopt ready-made community solutions (Claude Code + a harness), cheap and quick to fit, and only need to watch effect metrics such as alignment rounds and adoption rate; large organizations must split into small teams and standardize step by step. The roundtable also gives a measurement tip: during rollout watch penetration and AI-code share; after it spreads, watch requirements throughput per person and delivered value, and observe by requirement tier (L1 assistance through L4 end-to-end delivery) — **use the wrong metric, and AI stops at "the individual is faster."**

## 5.7 Two Paths and Three Kinds of Guardrails: Putting Uncertainty Inside the System

There is more than one way to land "AI-native." An academic essay in the *Harvard Data Science Review* relocates the source of efficiency: to unlock the 2–10x productivity potential of agents, enterprises must redesign workflows rather than treat agents as accelerators bolted onto existing processes<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#8-99">[8-99]</a></sup>. The Linde case it cites cut audit-report preparation time by 92% — the leverage was not the model but converting a serial process dominated by hand-offs and waiting into one that agents push forward continuously while humans intervene at key checkpoints. Conversely, the reasons pilots stall are highly consistent: an analysis in *Supply Chain Management Review* notes that AI tools are typically "layered" on top of legacy infrastructure and existing processes without redefining the process itself, so demos look good but never reach the P&L<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#8-100">[8-100]</a></sup>.

In pushing AI from "demo" into "daily routine," the first bottleneck is usually not the model but the toolchain itself. Linear's engineering retrospective is a concrete sample: once agents made writing code faster, CI immediately became the bottleneck — despite the test suite nearly quadrupling since the start of the year, Linear cut pull-request wait time from just over six minutes to just over five, by moving to faster runners, optimizing the jobs that gate other work, and reducing repeated environment setup<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#8-105">[8-105]</a></sup>. **When the producing end speeds up, the validating end becomes the new constraint — a narrow gate every "AI efficiency" narrative must pass through.**

Stripe turned that gate into an institution. Its knowledge AI platform, Kai, reached most of the company within two weeks of launch and now has 83% weekly active users (nearly all of GTM). Its design premise is a judgment: coding work could be handed to agents because compilers, tests, and git supply decades of "fast, verifiable guardrails," while knowledge work has almost no such structure and must build its own<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#8-101">[8-101]</a></sup>. Stripe therefore defined explicit invariants — for example, that data from two unrelated customer contexts must never appear in a single analysis — with an isolation boundary framed not as "what can this person access given their authorization" but as "what should this task be allowed to see given its context." Its earlier lesson is equally worth copying: a NoCode agent builder produced more than 4,000 micro-agents of uneven quality that became hard to monitor and maintain, and was eventually consolidated into a platform.

At the infrastructure layer, the answer is standardizing. Tencent Cloud (Chinese: 腾讯云) draws the distinction sharply: microservices are "one service for N users," homogeneous and stateless, while agents are "one agent for one user," heterogeneous, stateful, and autonomous — so elasticity (cold starts, horizontal scaling, cost flexibility), operations (a "pet" model, the opposite of microservices), and governance (unpredictable behavior) all require a purpose-built runtime<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#8-103">[8-103]</a></sup>. On the delivery side there is a more mundane lesson: a retrospective drawn from manufacturing and retail clients groups the costs invisible on the quote into three — volatility in model-call fees, context-window inflation, and multi-module orchestration and operations — noting that going from a single-module demo to five to eight production modules makes fault localization complexity grow on the order of N²<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#8-104">[8-104]</a></sup>.

Finally, the human side. A closed-door session at the ITValue Digital Value Summit recorded an uncomfortable observation: at Zhishishengong (智识神工), nearly all code is now generated by AI — but when AI coding sharply reduced the team's workload, many members did not shift toward systems analysis, documentation, and knowledge creation as expected. Instead they showed "all-round capability regression"<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#8-102">[8-102]</a></sup>. Its founder's judgment is that AI is not a tool but a new labor subject, and that the future organization is a "human-machine union" (CHR) of people plus multi-robot teams — humans managing, deciding, and creating knowledge, robots handling systems analysis, coding, and testing. He also left a line worth pasting on the wall of every transformation project: "The bullet of the revolution hits the revolutionaries first."

**When agents actually enter business systems, "turning the GUI into a command line" is far from enough.** Alibaba Cloud CIO Jiang Linquan (Chinese: 蒋林泉) breaks the path into two easily underestimated things<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#8-117">[8-117]</a></sup>. The first is permission control: a human operating item by item in a GUI is slow, but an agent with excessive permissions can read and rewrite in bulk automatically — "a human can only do limited damage in the GUI, but an agent's destructive power is ten times greater" — so the principle is that **the data an agent can see, the systems it can call, and the processes it can rewrite must not exceed the user's own permissions**; datasets need fine-grained row- and column-level control, plus auditing so every operation is traceable. The second is "migrating the guiding information": when a human operates a screen, field meanings, selectable ranges, and hover hints implicitly tell them what to do next; once the system is opened to agents, this implicit business semantics must be reorganized into fields, ranges, constraints, and exception handling that AI can understand — Jiang Linquan calls this the **"fence" (围栏)**. The fence also reserves a place for human intervention under uncertainty: "without guidance, AI will pick for you at random, and afterward you won't even know where it went wrong." The interface rework itself is quick (wrapping APIs as MCP took little time); what consumed months was permissions and semantics. **This is 5.1's "redesign first, automate second" projected onto the system layer: before opening the door to an agent, write into the system itself what it may do and how to do it right.**

## 5.8 Putting Agents Inside the Engineering Loop: Four Companies' Practice

The previous sections described how to shut uncertainty inside a system. This section looks at how four companies actually did it — and what they share is that none of them treated "AI productivity" as a tooling problem rather than a design problem of process and accountability.

**Calendly: the loop has durable state, and human checkpoints sit at the right nodes.** This scheduling SaaS company wove agents through the whole R&D flow: a Jira ticket or one-page brief defines the problem, constraints and acceptance criteria; an agent reviews it for ambiguity and missed edge cases; a planning agent breaks approved work into PR-sized tasks; implementation agents run in parallel; QA and review agents verify against requirements and the test suite; only then do humans review, approve and merge. Crucially, none of this lives in a disposable chat window — requirements stay in Jira, review history in GitHub, verification results in CI, and behavioral constraints in repository instructions<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#7-87">[7-87]</a></sup>. The hardest sample came from the IAM team: roughly 64 PRs in a week and a half (37 merged, 27 in review), covering about 30% of a project to decouple the IAM data model from the monolith, all running under a dedicated Service Identity and still required to pass two human review gates. One signal matters more than the speed: an internal tool let two team members who had never really shipped code — a designer and a product manager — create and deliver PRs. **Where guardrails and review are reliable enough, the boundary of who can safely participate in software development gets redrawn.** They summarized the shift in one quotable line: the bottleneck moved from writing code to alignment to judgment — "handing a vague ticket to a very fast agent does not get you a correct result faster, only a wrong result faster."

**Plaid: turn "things that must not happen" into mechanical blocks, not documentation.** All agent configuration is committed to the repository alongside human documentation, so the team shares one environment. Permission and Hooks enforce mechanically — reads allowed by default, `git push` asking every time, `rm -rf` refused outright; formatting and type checks after edits; protected-branch pushes blocked and secrets scanned with secretlint before commit. AGENTS.md loads automatically at session start (detailed material lives in docs, with only an index kept in the file), rules trigger by file path, and Skills and Subagents freeze recurring flows while isolating high-volume intermediate work like review and verification into separate contexts (a db-reader subagent, for instance, is restricted to read-only by hooks). The result: a five-person team moved from roughly 150 pull requests a month to roughly 600<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#7-91">[7-91]</a></sup>. Their own summary is worth copying: "the era of reading code is not over, but what you read has changed — you read how intent and constraints were designed, where the judgment boundary sits, and whether the process is reproducible."

**AWS's consulting team: split team-wide rollout into five steps, in order.** Supporting one development organization's move from individual use to multi-team rollout, they hit four problems with one root cause — output quality depended on personal prompting, agents did not follow established processes, individual know-how was not shared, and review and security verification had never entered the system; all four amounted to "the agent doesn't know how the team works." The buildout ran in five steps: make the rules explicit (fix every repeatedly-explained convention into a file, and always state what "done" means — without a completion criterion an agent reports success as soon as it compiles and skips the tests) → split knowledge and separate roles (keep only prohibitions and musts in always-read files, move detail into on-demand skills; separate implementation and review subagents, with at most two retries before a task goes to a human) → structure the workflow (the main agent only orchestrates and does not implement; inventory assets on a "before/after the task × human/AI" grid, which revealed that the most-missing quadrant was mechanical post-hoc verification of AI output) → add quality gates (decidable pass/fail, with failure barring entry to the next stage; gates admit only machine-decidable criteria and leave things like UX to humans; run a fixed "eval set" repeatedly rather than relying on a feeling that things got better) → close the learning loop (Hooks record correction instructions at session end as learning candidates, and scaffolding that is no longer needed is periodically removed)<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#8-123">[8-123]</a></sup>.

**Tencent Cloud's delivery retrospective: knowledge cold start eats 40-60% of the time.** Drawing on customer cases in automotive manufacturing, hotels, pharmaceutical retail and logistics, Tencent Cloud splits the path from PoC to production into four phases: knowledge cold start (RAG foundations), workflow orchestration (intent and execution), multi-agent collaboration, and governance and operations. The most underestimated number is that **knowledge cold start typically consumes 40-60% of total deployment time**, with highly repetitive failure modes — fragmented formats, mechanical text splitting that loses context, tabular data collapsing into unstructured text, and hard platform ceilings on file size. On the multi-agent side, one hotel group's three specialized agents (internal service, store operations, regional management) cover more than 5,000 hotels and 20 brands, lifting business-scenario coverage from 75% to 100%, cutting FAQ maintenance from over 1,000 entries to just over 100, and reducing new store managers' operational error rate by 60%. On the governance side, one number reminds everyone that cost must be visible: a dairy company's copywriting agent consumes 30 million tokens a day<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#8-125">[8-125]</a></sup>. Together the four companies suggest one shared test: **replace the question "can we use AI here?" with "what must a machine verify mechanically, and what must a human sign?"**

One further practice deserves its own paragraph, because it is the precondition that makes the four cases above possible. A roundtable of engineers from Taobao Shangou, NetEase CodeWave and Ping An Technology set out three increasingly clear layers of guardrails: the first turns requirements from prose into structure (SPEC defining goals and constraints, EARS-style sentences turning requirements into verifiable items), the second turns "done" into an executable gate through testing and CI, and the third unifies Skills, Lint, CI and a team constitution into a single "constitution" every agent reads — using tools such as ArchUnit and PMD to turn rules written in Markdown into builds that actually fail<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#8-129">[8-129]</a></sup>. Two of their judgments are worth recording alongside: high-complexity, high-precision scenarios are unsuitable for vibe coding; and "the first victims of vibe coding have already appeared — they need to hire vibe-coding code repair specialists." **Rules only count as followed by agents once they are executable** — the implicit precondition of all four cases in this section.

## 5.9 Semi-Black-Box Code: Moving Observability from Troubleshooting into Development

This section covers an often-ignored consequence: the speed of producing code has risen, but the speed at which people understand the system has not risen with it.

An engineer who has long worked on monitoring and observability named this state the "semi-black box" — the code is not an unreadable black box, yet implementation details, exception paths, historical compatibility logic and behavior under real traffic can no longer be derived inside an engineer's head<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#8-127">[8-127]</a></sup>. Semantic errors are worse: AI readily produces code that is syntactically correct, passes tests, and is not quite right in business terms — and that kind of problem "looks approximately fine," making it the hardest to catch in review. His prescription is to move observability forward, from "look at metrics and logs when production breaks" to part of design, development, review, canary release and release verification:

- every important change ships with its observability: what the critical path is, what metrics a normal case should produce, what logs an exception should emit, whether a trace shows which branch was taken, and whether there is a business-level success rate and latency;
- release is not the end but the start of verification — error rates, latency, resource consumption, downstream dependencies and core business metrics all need to be watched after a change, with anomalies attributable to it quickly;
- engineers must prove themselves with an evidence chain: when the version shipped, whether the error rate changed, whether P95/P99 changed, where the trace stalls, where anomalous log parameters come from, whether configuration changed, whether there are slow queries, whether dependencies are flapping.

This is two faces of the same thing as the guardrails in 5.7: guardrails define what is not allowed, observability defines what entitles you to say it is correct. **Tests prove it can probably run before release; observability proves it is actually running correctly in the real world after release.**

LangChain founder Harrison Chase gives the isomorphic judgment from the agent side: in an agent application, behavior is determined not only by code but also by the model, a black box running outside the code, so the "source of truth" shifts from code to code plus traces — you do not know what is in context at step 14, because the previous 13 steps could have pulled in anything; and precisely for that reason online testing often matters more than offline testing, since behavior only emerges under real inputs<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#8-128">[8-128]</a></sup>. He adds one line entirely aligned with this section: when something goes wrong on a team, the first reaction is no longer "show me the code" but "send us that trace."

## 5.10 Review Becomes the New Bottleneck: More Code, Not More Software

Sections 5.8 and 5.9 describe how to put agents in the right place; this section looks at where the bill lands once they are there.

**A study covering more than 700 firms pushes the efficiency account downstream.** Harvard researchers Fiona Chen and James Stratton used Jellyfish engineering-productivity data covering more than 700 software firms, over 700,000 employees and roughly 300 million work events (commits, pull requests and the like) from 2021 through March 2026. The finding has two halves. Capacity did rise: after coding agents were introduced, total lines of code rose 30% on average, commits rose 20% and pull requests rose 23%. Output did not follow: the resolution rate of Jira issues and epics showed no statistically significant change, and the size and complexity of those work items did not shift structurally either<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#1-44">[1-44]</a></sup>.

**The increment was absorbed by review.** After agents arrived, the average review time from pull request to merge stretched 49%, the share of pull requests requiring changes nearly doubled and comments per pull request rose 35%. At the same time the share of workers performing reviews rose 14% while total active headcount did not change significantly — the organization absorbed "more code coming in" with "more people looking." AI review did not close the gap: by March 2026, 80% of sampled firms used some form of AI code review, yet agents produced only 23.3% of review comments and touched 10.8% of pull requests, leaving the overwhelming majority of judgment to people; 95% of firms had already deployed coding agents.

**The capacity ceiling is no longer on the generation side.** Calendly's summary in 5.8 — that the bottleneck moved from writing code to alignment and judgment — now has firm-level quantification; the observation in 4.9 that end-to-end demand consumed no fewer people is the same phenomenon at the other end. Three directions follow. Make "what counts as done" a machine-decidable gate so rework happens before submission rather than inside review. Layer the review: formatting, typing, tests and static checks to machines, semantic judgment and accountability to people. And treat reviewers as capacity to be staffed rather than slack to be drawn on. **When the cost of generation approaches zero, the scarcest resource in the organization is the time of people willing to sign their names.**

## 5.11 The Core Judgment of This Chapter

**Workflow redesign is the minimal executable unit of the AI-native organization.** Organizational form (Chapter 4) is the skeleton; workflow redesign is the muscle — an organization doesn't need to finish "organizational restructuring" before becoming AI-native; it only needs to start with one workflow: map the real process, delete redundant steps, define decision rights, design for production, measure the whole flow, and iterate in 90-day cycles. **Reduce organizational friction (the denominator) first, then multiply talent and AI leverage (the numerator).** In the next chapter, we take on the hardest variable: people.
